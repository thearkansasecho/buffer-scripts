## Images researched and not used, and why

- Portrait of Daisy Bates in Paul J. Scheips, *The Role of Federal Military Forces in Domestic Disorders, 1945–1992* (U.S. Army Center of Military History), book p. 29. The book is a U.S. government work, but the illustration list credits **page 29 to Corbis**, a commercial agency. Not used.
- Library of Congress NAACP Visual Materials photographs of Bates and the Little Rock Nine in her living room (e.g. LC-DIG-ppmsca-37809; photographer Cecil Layne) and related NAACP lots. Rights advisory: “Publication may be restricted.” Not used.
- LIFE / Thomas McAvoy photographs in the Wisconsin Historical Society Daisy Bates papers (including Bates at the window with the 101st). Restricted; LIFE-owned. Not used.
- Encyclopedia of Arkansas and Arkansas History Commission / University of Arkansas Special Collections “courtesy of” portraits. Rights not cleared. Not used.
- 2026 historical-marker news photographs (Arkansas Times / Brian Chilson and others). News photographs, not licensed to Echo. Not used. The new marker is mentioned in narration and typography only, over the NPS house still.
- The 2024 U.S. Capitol statue of Bates (sculptor Benjamin Victor). Official photographs of the statue do not clear the sculpture’s copyright. Not used.
- NARA photograph of Eisenhower, Faubus, and Brooks Hays at Newport, Sept. 1957 (NARA 7865613). Public domain, but the picture records a conference *before* the troop order and could be misread as the moment Eisenhower sent the 101st. Not used.
- Modern CC BY-SA photographs of Central High and Daisy L. Gatson Bates Drive. The brief asked for archival or public-domain stills; official NPS and Army photographs were used instead.

No usable public-domain or clearly licensed portrait of Daisy Bates herself was found. The short therefore does not invent her face and does not use a restricted portrait. The house, the school, and the 1957 Army escort photograph carry the picture.

## Fact sources for the narration (claim by claim)

Spoken script:

- **“Daisy Bates was born on Veterans Day.”** / **“By most records, Daisy Lee Gatson Bates was born November 11th, 1914, in Huttig, Arkansas.”** CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Daisy Lee Gatson Bates (1914?–1999),” last updated October 12, 2026: “the date of her birth is usually given as November 11, 1914”; the entry title hedges the year as 1914?. https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/daisy-lee-gatson-bates-591/ National Park Service people page states the same date and Huttig without the hedge: https://www.nps.gov/people/daisy-lee-gatson-bates.htm The voiceover follows the EOA hedge (“by most records”) as required. Veterans Day falls on November 11.
- **“She and her husband, L.C., published the Arkansas State Press.”** NPS: co-owner, with her husband L.C., of the Arkansas State Press. EOA: they moved to Little Rock in 1941 to start the weekly; married March 4, 1942, in Fordyce; she was named city editor in 1945. The VO says they published it, not that she was the sole publisher or a frequent bylined writer (EOA: “she rarely wrote for the paper”).
- **“She led the state NAACP.”** NPS: selected in 1952 as president of the state conference of NAACP Arkansas branches. EOA: elected president of the Arkansas Conference of Branches in 1952; remained until 1961.
- **“When nine Black teenagers set out to integrate Central High, they started the day at her house — and they ended it there.”** NPS house page: throughout the crisis the Bates home “became the official pick-up and drop-off site for the Little Rock Nine’s trips to and from Central High School each school day.” EOA museum entry: the home “served as a safe place for the Little Rock Nine… to prepare for school and to return to afterward.” Arkansas Times, Arielle Robinson, February 17, 2026: the Nine “would meet with Bates at her house each day before school and were taken to Central High and then dropped back off at the Bates home.” https://arktimes.com/arkansas-blog/2026/02/17/new-historical-marker-unveiled-at-daisy-bates-home-honors-civil-rights-legends-desegregation-work
- **“A rock came through the living-room window with a note: Stone this time. Dynamite next.”** NPS house page, exact wording: “In August 1957, a rock crashed through the living room window with a note attached that read ‘STONE THIS TIME. DYNAMITE NEXT.’” Arkansas Times (Feb. 17, 2026) independently describes a rock through the living-room window with a note saying dynamite would be next.
- **“Crosses burned in the yard. Shots were fired from a passing car.”** NPS house page: “Two crosses were burned in the front yard on different occasions and shots were fired into the house from a passing car.” Arkansas Times agrees (burning crosses; shooting at the house).
- **“The house became a fortress.”** NPS house page, quoted language: over the 1957–58 school year, “this home ‘became a fortress’” with a volunteer guard committee, flood lights, and frequent calls to the police.
- **“On September 25th, 1957, after President Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne, those nine students walked into Central High under Army escort.”** NPS people page: on September 25, the day after Eisenhower ordered the 101st Airborne into Little Rock and federalized the Arkansas National Guard, the students were escorted safely into the school. Army history (Scheips, p. 29 chapter and later Little Rock chapter) and NARA’s “60 Years On: The Little Rock Nine” (Aug. 30, 2017) agree on September 25 as the first full day under airborne escort.
- **“They had been picked up at Daisy Bates’s door.”** Scheips, *The Role of Federal Military Forces in Domestic Disorders, 1945–1992*, official U.S. Army Center of Military History: “the black students had been picked up in an Army station wagon with jeep escorts at the home of Daisy Bates, head of the NAACP in Little Rock.” Then twenty-two airborne soldiers escorted them through the front entrance; the same general procedure returned them at the end of the day. Internet Archive: https://archive.org/details/CMHPub3020
- **“That house is a National Historic Landmark.”** NPS people page (designated 2002) and NPS place page (Designation: National Historic Landmark). EOA museum entry notes National Historic Landmark status registered January 3, 2001. The VO does not pick a year, because those two dates differ; the landmark status itself is not in dispute.
- **“This year, Arkansas put a new marker there.”** EOA, “L. C. and Daisy Bates Museum,” last updated February 25, 2026: on February 17, 2026, the day after the official state day commemorating Daisy Bates, a historical marker was unveiled at the house as part of the 250th anniversary commemoration. https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/l-c-and-daisy-bates-museum-6073/ Confirmed by Arkansas Times, February 17, 2026 (Arkansas 250 Commission marker). “This year” is correct for a 2026 posting.
- **“The command post is still standing.”** NPS place page: the home is “nationally significant for its role as the de facto command post during the Central High School desegregation crisis” and, while privately owned, is open to the public as a museum. Arkansas Times and the Arkansas Advocate also use “command post.” The hook’s “war room” is figurative language for that documented role, not a quoted label from 1957.

## Facts researched and intentionally omitted or softened

- Birth-parent names and the murder of her mother (EOA and NPS). True and sourced, but heavier than this short needed, and EOA notes that the identity of her birth parents “has not been conclusively established.”
- Exact marriage date (March 4, 1942) and the 1963 divorce and remarriage (EOA).
- Advertising boycott and the 1959 closing of the State Press (NPS, EOA).
- March on Washington speaking role (EOA); Mitchellville antipoverty work; 2024 Capitol statue.
- State holiday (third Monday in February). The new marker is the timely 2026 fact; the holiday is in the sources but not in the VO.
- MLK and Thurgood Marshall visits (EOA museum; Arkansas Times). True, left out to keep the short on the Nine and the house.
- “War room” is the assigned hook metaphor. Body copy uses sourced “command post” and “fortress.”
