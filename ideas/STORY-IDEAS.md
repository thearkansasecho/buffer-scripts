# Vetted story ideas

These are research leads, not finished scripts. Every hook below is limited
to facts found in the sources listed. Open those pages again before you
write a voiceover. If a public-domain image is not confirmed, do not invent
one, and do not treat an Encyclopedia of Arkansas “courtesy of” photograph
as free to use.

Timely hooks researched and source-checked on November 12, 2026

### T1. Sgt. Ulysses Grant Baugh Sr.’s POW food bowl — “A Soldier’s Story”

**Hook:** A food bowl that kept a Black Arkansas soldier alive in a Korean
War POW camp is now in a free Little Rock exhibit. Mosaic Templars Cultural
Center opened “A Soldier’s Story: The Legacy of African American Service
Members” on November 10, 2026. It runs through March 20, 2027. Executive
Director Key Fletcher said Sgt. Ulysses Grant Baugh Sr. used the bowl as a
lifeline for food and drink and as an anchor through imprisonment, then
came home and “didn’t share that story a whole lot.” The exhibit is
community-sourced: families of Arkansas veterans lent private artifacts so
the stories could be told.

**Sources (verified):**

- Nathan Treece, “Mosaic Templars Cultural Center opens ‘A Soldier’s
  Story,’” Little Rock Public Radio / KUAR, November 10, 2026.
  https://www.ualrpublicradio.org/local-regional-news/2026-11-10/mosaic-templars-cultural-center-opens-a-soldiers-story
  (page opened November 12, 2026.)

**Public-domain image lead:** The bowl and other artifacts are family
loans on exhibit, not free images. Do not photograph them for a post
without the museum’s and the family’s permission. For a recap, point
viewers to the free exhibit rather than lifting object photos.

### T2. Daisy Bates, November 11, 1914, the Bates house, and the 2026 marker

**Hook:** The date usually given for Daisy Lee Gatson Bates’s birth is
November 11, 1914, in Huttig (Union County). The National Park Service
uses that date. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas still prints the year with
a question mark and says it is “usually given as November 11, 1914,” so
say it that way in a voiceover. She and L. C. Bates published the
*Arkansas State Press* and mentored the Little Rock Nine from their home
at 1207 W. 28th Street — now the L. C. and Daisy Bates Museum, a
National Historic Landmark. On February 17, 2026, a historical marker
was unveiled at the house as part of the national 250th-anniversary
commemoration.

**Sources (verified):**

- U.S. National Park Service, “Daisy Lee Gatson Bates.” Place of birth:
  Huttig, Arkansas; date of birth: November 11, 1914.
  https://www.nps.gov/people/daisy-lee-gatson-bates.htm
- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “L. C. and Daisy Bates Museum.” Marker
  unveiled February 17, 2026.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/l-c-and-daisy-bates-museum-6073/
- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Daisy Lee Gatson Bates (1914?–1999),”
  for the caution on the birth year.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/daisy-lee-gatson-bates-591/

**Public-domain image lead:** Not confirmed. Photographs and *State Press*
pages from the 1940s–1950s are likely still under copyright. Prefer a
cleared image of the house or the 2026 marker with permission, not an
AI face and not a famous copyrighted 1957 news photograph.

### T4. Archer Shepperson — marker unveiled October 17, 2026

**Hook:** On October 17, 2026, a state historical marker was unveiled at
Archer Shepperson’s grave in Washington Cemetery, 124 Carrigan Street,
Washington, Arkansas — 121 years to the day after his death (October 17,
1905, per EOA). Born into enslavement in Arkansas on February 7, 1850,
he was a deputy sheriff, served in the Arkansas General Assembly in 1873
as a Republican from the Fourteenth District at age 23, later sat on the
Hempstead County Board of Supervisors, and spent 25 years as principal
of the public school for Black students. A descendant, Shepperson A.
Wilbun Jr., was there for the unveiling.

**Sources (verified):**

- Josh Williams, “Arkansas Historical Marker sign—Archer Shepperson,”
  Hope Prescott, October 19, 2026. Includes the marker text.
  https://hopeprescott.com/2026/10/19/arkansas-historical-marker-sign-archer-shepperson/
- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Archer Shepperson (1850–1905).”
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/archer-shepperson-20400/

**Public-domain image lead:** The 2026 marker and the earlier stone at
the grave are modern or early-twentieth-century objects; a photo of the
marker needs the photographer’s permission (EOA’s grave photo is
courtesy of Peggy Lloyd). Look for a pre-1930 newspaper engraving or a
legislative page before using a face.

### T5. Joseph Carter Corbin, Old Main, and Branch Normal / UAPB

**Hook:** The man who signed the contract for University Hall — Old Main,
the first building at the University of Arkansas in Fayetteville — was
Joseph Carter Corbin, Arkansas’s superintendent of public instruction
from 1873 to 1875 and, by virtue of that office, president of the
university’s board of trustees. He also helped secure the 1873
legislation for Branch Normal College and, in 1875, became its principal
in Pine Bluff. That school is now the University of Arkansas at Pine
Bluff.

**Sources (verified):**

- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Joseph Carter Corbin (1833–1911).”
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/joseph-carter-corbin-1624/
- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “University of Arkansas at Pine Bluff
  (UAPB).”
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/university-of-arkansas-at-pine-bluff-4136/

**Public-domain image lead:** The engraved portrait in William J.
Simmons, *Men of Mark* (1887), the same public-domain volume used for

### T6. Thanksgiving genealogy: the 1950 census and the Freedmen’s Bureau

**Hook:** Thanksgiving is when families already tell stories. Two free,
official starting points can turn a name at the table into a record: the
1950 U.S. Census (every household, searchable by name and address) and
the Freedmen’s Bureau records, which document formerly enslaved people
and their families after the Civil War, including in Arkansas. This is a
how-to piece, not a claim about any one family. Do not invent ancestors
or results.

**Sources (verified):**

- U.S. National Archives, 1950 Census.
  https://1950census.archives.gov/
- U.S. National Archives, “African American Heritage: The Freedmen’s
  Bureau.”
  https://www.archives.gov/research/african-americans/freedmens-bureau

**Public-domain image lead:** Blank or sample pages from those federal
records (U.S. government works) — a census schedule or a Bureau form —
credited to the National Archives. Never fill them in with made-up
names presented as real people.

### T7. Mosaic Templars of America origin, and the December 6 market

the 1920s it had an estimated 100,000 members in twenty-six states, the
Caribbean, and South and Central America. Tell the origin; invite people
to the market and the museum. Do not invent a December 6 founding date —
the sources give 1882 and 1883 (articles of incorporation filed May 22,
1883, in a second reference work).

### T10. John H. Johnson of Arkansas City — *Ebony* and *Jet*

**Hook:** Johnny Johnson was born January 19, 1918, in Arkansas City
(Desha County). In 1942, using his mother’s furniture as collateral for
a $500 loan, he mailed the first issue of *Negro Digest*. *Ebony*
followed in 1945 and *Jet* in 1951, and Johnson Publishing Company
became the largest Black-owned publishing company in the world. Arkansas
now marks November 1 as John H. Johnson Day; a museum and a planned
state park carry his name in the town where he started.

**Sources (verified):**

- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “John H. Johnson (1918–2005).” Last
  updated November 3, 2026; page opened November 12, 2026.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/john-harold-johnson-2354/
- Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture,
  “75 Years of Ebony Magazine.”
  https://nmaahc.si.edu/75-years-ebony-magazine

**Public-domain image lead:** Not confirmed. Mid-century portraits and
magazine covers are likely still under copyright. The Arkansas City
museum or John H. Johnson Park may have a rights-cleared image after
permission. Never generate his face.

## 1. Florence Beatrice Smith Price (1887–1953)

**Hook:** Little Rock would not let her into the Arkansas State Music
Teachers Association because of her race. A few years after she left for
Chicago, the Chicago Symphony Orchestra played her Symphony in E Minor —
the first time a major American orchestra performed a Black woman’s
symphonic work (June 15, 1933).

**Sources (verified):**

- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Florence Price (1887–1953).” Born in
  Little Rock on April 9, 1887; denied membership in the Arkansas State
  Music Teachers Association; Chicago Symphony premiere on June 15, 1933
  under Frederick Stock.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/florence-beatrice-smith-price-1742/
  (HTTP 200, opened November 12, 2026.)
- Linda W. Reese, “Florence Beatrice Smith Price (1887-1953),” BlackPast.
  https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/price-florence-beatrice-smith-1887-1953/

**Public-domain image lead:** Not confirmed. A mid-century portrait
circulates, and EOA has a circa 1930 media entry, but Echo does not use
“courtesy of” photographs without a rights check, and a 1930s–1940s
photograph is not automatically public domain. Do not generate a face.
Hold the picture until a pre-1930 publication or a cleared archive still
is in hand; a concert program may be used only after its own rights are
checked. Do not illustrate her with cotton-field imagery, including art
for the 1928 piece title *At the Cotton Gin*.

## 2. Josephine Irvin Harris Pankey (1869–1954)

**Hook:** In Jim Crow Little Rock, restrictive covenants limited where
Black families could buy land, and banks often would not finance them.
Starting in 1907 she bought the land herself — first eighty acres west of
downtown — and built a community that still bears her name.

**Sources (verified):**

- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Josephine Irvin Harris Pankey
  (1869–1954).” First land purchase in 1907: eighty acres, thirteen miles
  west of downtown Little Rock, $400; later additions followed.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/josephine-irvin-harris-pankey-9144/
  (HTTP 200, opened November 12, 2026.)
- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Pankey (Pulaski County).” The community
  is one of three she owned and subdivided, and the one that remains
  intact.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/pankey-pulaski-county-5934/

**Public-domain image lead:** No confirmed public-domain portrait. EOA
photographs of Josephine and Samuel Pankey are credited “Courtesy of
Raymond Douglas Sr.” and are not treated as free. A period plat or land
record for the Pankey additions is the better lead, if a specific public
filing can be located and credited. Do not use a modern gravestone photo
as if it were a lifetime portrait.

## 3. “Sister Rosetta” Tharpe (1915–1973)

**Hook:** Before later household names of rock and roll, a guitar player
from Cotton Plant, Arkansas, had already recorded for a major label and
walked onto the Carnegie Hall stage. EOA identifies her as one of gospel’s
first superstars, the first gospel performer to record for a major label
(Decca, 1938), and a cited influence on artists including Arkansan
Johnny Cash. She appeared in John Hammond’s “From Spirituals to Swing” at
Carnegie Hall on December 23, 1938.

**Sources (verified):**

- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “'Sister Rosetta' Tharpe (1915–1973).”
  Born in Cotton Plant (Woodruff County) on March 20, 1915; Decca, 1938;
  Carnegie Hall, December 23, 1938.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/sister-rosetta-tharpe-1781/
- CALS Encyclopedia of Arkansas, “Gospel Music,” which independently
  places her in that Carnegie Hall program and discusses her gospel-to-
  secular crossover.
  https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/gospel-music-4978/

**Public-domain image lead:** Not confirmed to Echo’s standard. A 1938
publicity photograph is sometimes listed as public domain on Wikimedia
Commons under a no-notice claim; verify that claim before using it. EOA
and hall-of-fame portraits are courtesy images, not automatically free.
Naming the town **Cotton Plant** is allowed (it is the place name). Do
not use cotton-field imagery.

## 13. Edith Irby Jones (1927–2019)

**Hook:** In 1948 she became the first African American student admitted

graduated in 1952, practiced and taught for decades, and in 1985 was
elected the first female president of the National Medical Association.
